# Hypothyroidism

## Overview
Hypothyroidism (underactive thyroid) occurs when the thyroid gland does not make enough thyroid hormone, slowing many body functions. In iodine-sufficient countries such as the United States, the most common cause is Hashimoto's thyroiditis, an autoimmune disease. Other causes include thyroid surgery, radioactive iodine treatment, certain medications such as lithium and amiodarone, and iodine deficiency worldwide.

## Symptoms
Symptoms develop slowly and include fatigue, feeling cold, weight gain, constipation, dry skin, thinning hair, slowed heart rate, depression, memory problems, muscle aches, and heavy or irregular menstrual periods. An enlarged thyroid (goiter) may occur.

## Diagnosis
The thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) blood test is the best initial screening test. In primary hypothyroidism, TSH is high and free thyroxine (free T4) is low. Subclinical hypothyroidism means TSH is elevated while free T4 is normal. Thyroid peroxidase (TPO) antibodies support a diagnosis of Hashimoto's thyroiditis.

## Treatment
The standard treatment is levothyroxine, a synthetic form of T4, taken daily. It should be taken on an empty stomach, typically 30 to 60 minutes before breakfast, with water. Calcium, iron supplements, and antacids should be separated from levothyroxine by about 4 hours because they reduce its absorption. TSH is rechecked about 6 to 8 weeks after starting treatment or changing the dose. Treatment is usually lifelong. Too much levothyroxine can cause symptoms of hyperthyroidism, such as palpitations, anxiety, insomnia, and bone loss.
