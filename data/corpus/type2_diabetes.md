# Type 2 Diabetes

## Overview
Type 2 diabetes is a chronic condition in which the body becomes resistant to insulin or does not produce enough insulin, leading to high blood glucose. It accounts for about 90-95% of diabetes cases and is strongly associated with excess body weight and physical inactivity.

## Symptoms
Symptoms often develop slowly and may include increased thirst (polydipsia), frequent urination (polyuria), increased hunger, fatigue, blurred vision, slow-healing sores, frequent infections, and tingling or numbness in the hands or feet. Many people have no symptoms at diagnosis.

## Diagnosis
Diabetes is diagnosed by any of the following:
- Hemoglobin A1C of 6.5% or higher.
- Fasting plasma glucose of 126 mg/dL (7.0 mmol/L) or higher after at least 8 hours without food.
- 2-hour plasma glucose of 200 mg/dL or higher during a 75 g oral glucose tolerance test.
- A random plasma glucose of 200 mg/dL or higher in a person with classic symptoms of hyperglycemia.

Prediabetes is defined as an A1C of 5.7% to 6.4% or a fasting glucose of 100 to 125 mg/dL. The A1C test reflects average blood glucose over the previous 2 to 3 months.

## Treatment
Lifestyle changes, including weight loss of 5-7% of body weight, a healthy eating pattern, and at least 150 minutes of moderate activity per week, are foundational. Metformin is generally the first-line medication. Common side effects of metformin include gastrointestinal upset such as diarrhea and nausea; it is rarely associated with lactic acidosis. Other options include SGLT2 inhibitors (such as empagliflozin), GLP-1 receptor agonists (such as semaglutide), DPP-4 inhibitors, sulfonylureas, and insulin. SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists are preferred for people with established cardiovascular disease, heart failure, or chronic kidney disease. A common A1C target for many adults is below 7%.

## Complications and Monitoring
Long-term complications include heart disease, stroke, kidney disease (diabetic nephropathy), nerve damage (neuropathy), eye damage (retinopathy), and foot ulcers. People with diabetes should have an annual dilated eye exam, regular foot exams, and yearly kidney function testing.

## Hypoglycemia
Low blood sugar (below 70 mg/dL) can occur with insulin or sulfonylureas. Symptoms include shakiness, sweating, confusion, and fast heartbeat. The "15-15 rule" is to take 15 grams of fast-acting carbohydrate and recheck blood sugar after 15 minutes.
