# Asthma

## Overview
Asthma is a chronic inflammatory disease of the airways that causes episodes of airway narrowing. The airways become swollen and produce extra mucus, and the surrounding muscles tighten, making it hard to breathe.

## Symptoms
Typical symptoms are wheezing, shortness of breath, chest tightness, and coughing, often worse at night or early morning. Symptoms vary over time and in intensity.

## Triggers
Common triggers include allergens (pollen, dust mites, pet dander, mold), respiratory infections, exercise, cold air, tobacco smoke, air pollution, strong odors, and certain medications such as aspirin and other NSAIDs in some people.

## Diagnosis
Diagnosis is based on symptom history and lung function tests. Spirometry measures how much and how fast a person can exhale; an improvement in FEV1 of at least 12% (and 200 mL) after a bronchodilator supports the diagnosis. Peak flow meters can be used at home to monitor airflow.

## Treatment
Treatment uses two types of medication:
- Controller (maintenance) medications reduce airway inflammation over time. Inhaled corticosteroids (such as fluticasone or budesonide) are the most effective long-term controllers.
- Reliever (rescue) medications quickly open the airways during symptoms. Short-acting beta-agonists (SABA) such as albuterol work within minutes.

Current GINA guidance recommends that adults and adolescents not use SABA alone; a low-dose inhaled corticosteroid-formoterol combination (such as budesonide-formoterol) can be used as needed as a reliever. Needing a rescue inhaler more than twice a week suggests asthma is not well controlled. Rinsing the mouth after using an inhaled corticosteroid helps prevent oral thrush.

## Asthma Action Plan and Emergencies
Everyone with asthma should have a written asthma action plan. Seek emergency care if a rescue inhaler does not help, if breathing is very difficult, if lips or fingernails turn blue or gray, or if the person is too breathless to speak in full sentences.
