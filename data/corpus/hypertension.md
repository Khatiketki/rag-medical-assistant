# Hypertension (High Blood Pressure)

## Overview
Hypertension is a condition in which the force of blood against artery walls is consistently too high. Blood pressure is recorded as two numbers: systolic pressure (when the heart beats) over diastolic pressure (when the heart rests between beats), measured in millimeters of mercury (mmHg). It is often called a "silent killer" because most people have no symptoms even when readings are dangerously high.

## Blood Pressure Categories
Under the 2017 ACC/AHA guideline, adult blood pressure is classified as:
- Normal: systolic below 120 and diastolic below 80 mmHg.
- Elevated: systolic 120-129 and diastolic below 80 mmHg.
- Stage 1 hypertension: systolic 130-139 or diastolic 80-89 mmHg.
- Stage 2 hypertension: systolic 140 or higher, or diastolic 90 or higher.
- Hypertensive crisis: systolic above 180 and/or diastolic above 120 mmHg. A crisis with symptoms such as chest pain, shortness of breath, vision changes, or difficulty speaking requires emergency care.

Diagnosis is based on the average of two or more readings taken on two or more occasions. Home or ambulatory monitoring helps rule out "white coat hypertension".

## Risk Factors
Risk factors include older age, family history, obesity, physical inactivity, high sodium intake, excessive alcohol use, tobacco use, chronic kidney disease, diabetes, and obstructive sleep apnea.

## Complications
Uncontrolled hypertension damages blood vessels and organs and raises the risk of heart attack, stroke, heart failure, chronic kidney disease, and vision loss.

## Treatment
Lifestyle changes are first-line for everyone with elevated blood pressure: the DASH diet (rich in fruits, vegetables, whole grains, and low-fat dairy), limiting sodium to less than 2,300 mg per day (ideally about 1,500 mg), regular aerobic exercise of about 150 minutes per week, weight loss, limiting alcohol, and quitting smoking.

Common first-line medication classes are thiazide diuretics (such as chlorthalidone or hydrochlorothiazide), ACE inhibitors (such as lisinopril), angiotensin II receptor blockers or ARBs (such as losartan), and calcium channel blockers (such as amlodipine). A dry cough is a common side effect of ACE inhibitors. For most adults the treatment target is below 130/80 mmHg.
