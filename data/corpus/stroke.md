# Stroke

## Overview
A stroke occurs when blood supply to part of the brain is interrupted or reduced, depriving brain tissue of oxygen. About 87% of strokes are ischemic, caused by a blocked artery; hemorrhagic strokes are caused by bleeding in the brain. A transient ischemic attack (TIA), or "mini-stroke", causes temporary symptoms and is a warning sign of future stroke. A stroke is a medical emergency: call 911 immediately.

## Warning Signs: BE FAST
The BE FAST acronym helps recognize stroke:
- B - Balance: sudden loss of balance or coordination.
- E - Eyes: sudden vision loss or double vision.
- F - Face drooping: one side of the face droops or is numb; ask the person to smile.
- A - Arm weakness: one arm is weak or numb; ask the person to raise both arms.
- S - Speech difficulty: slurred or strange speech.
- T - Time to call 911: note the time when symptoms first appeared.

A sudden severe headache with no known cause may also signal a stroke.

## Emergency Treatment
For ischemic stroke, clot-dissolving medication (thrombolytics such as alteplase or tenecteplase) must generally be given within 4.5 hours of symptom onset. Mechanical thrombectomy can remove large clots, in selected patients up to 24 hours after onset. A CT scan is done first to rule out bleeding before thrombolytics are given. "Time is brain": about 1.9 million neurons are lost every minute during a large untreated stroke.

## Risk Factors and Prevention
High blood pressure is the leading modifiable risk factor for stroke. Other risk factors include atrial fibrillation, smoking, diabetes, high cholesterol, obesity, physical inactivity, and heavy alcohol use. People with atrial fibrillation often take anticoagulants to reduce stroke risk.
