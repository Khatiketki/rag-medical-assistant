# Gastroesophageal Reflux Disease (GERD)

## Overview
GERD occurs when stomach acid frequently flows back into the esophagus, irritating its lining. Occasional reflux is common; GERD is typically defined as reflux symptoms occurring at least twice a week or causing complications. A weakened lower esophageal sphincter and hiatal hernia contribute to reflux.

## Symptoms
The most common symptom is heartburn, a burning sensation in the chest that often occurs after eating and may be worse at night or when lying down. Other symptoms include regurgitation of food or sour liquid, difficulty swallowing, chronic cough, hoarseness, and a feeling of a lump in the throat.

## Risk Factors and Triggers
Risk factors include obesity, pregnancy, smoking, and hiatal hernia. Common dietary triggers are fatty or fried foods, chocolate, peppermint, caffeine, alcohol, carbonated beverages, citrus, tomato products, and spicy foods. Eating large meals or eating close to bedtime worsens symptoms.

## Treatment
Lifestyle measures include losing weight, avoiding trigger foods, avoiding meals 2 to 3 hours before lying down, elevating the head of the bed by 6 to 8 inches, and quitting smoking. Medications include antacids for quick relief, H2 blockers (such as famotidine), and proton pump inhibitors (PPIs, such as omeprazole and pantoprazole), which are the most effective. PPIs work best when taken 30 to 60 minutes before the first meal of the day. A typical initial PPI course is 8 weeks.

## Complications and Warning Signs
Chronic GERD can lead to esophagitis, esophageal strictures, and Barrett's esophagus, a precancerous change that increases the risk of esophageal adenocarcinoma. Alarm symptoms that warrant prompt evaluation with upper endoscopy include difficulty or painful swallowing, unintentional weight loss, vomiting blood, black stools, and anemia. Chest pain should be evaluated to rule out a heart problem.
